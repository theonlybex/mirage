# The algorithm under both creators' videos

The `vlog` and `episode` formulas record what David Dobrik and Mat Armstrong do. This is what sits underneath both: a procedure that doesn't need a Bugatti, a celebrity or a friend group.

**How it was derived.** From the title and view data of 769 videos, 48 thumbnails, a 9-video Dobrik breakdown, and a count of doubt, setback and win phrases in the captions of three Armstrong videos. Steps 1–4 and 8–10 are measured on both creators. The cadence numbers in steps 6–7 are measured on Armstrong only, by phrase counting, so they are approximate. Nothing here has been tested on your own videos yet.

Numbers marked "×" compare median views inside one channel: videos with that feature against that channel's videos without it. They show what goes with more views, not proof of cause.

## The one idea

Both channels sell the same thing: **one question the viewer can't answer without watching, about something they already care about, answered late and on a human face.**

- Armstrong: "Will this wrecked thing ever run again?"
- Dobrik: "How will this person react?"

The car and the celebrity are only ways of making the question big. Everything else exists to raise that question fast, keep it open, and pay it off.

## The procedure

### 1. Find a gap
A gap is something familiar shown in a state it shouldn't be in. The viewer must already know the normal state without being told.

| Creator | Familiar thing | Wrong state |
|---|---|---|
| Armstrong | A £1.7M yacht; a Bugatti | Rusting and dead; in two halves in a garage |
| Dobrik | A friend the audience knows | Handed a Lamborghini; sat next to Justin Bieber |

The gap's size is what "what he buys" was really measuring. His lowest series (a Lamborghini SUV for his girlfriend) cost plenty but had a small gap: nobody said it couldn't be done. A gap can be cheap: the worst-rated item, 300 saved pulls gone in ten minutes, a beginner against an expert, an app built in a day.

### 2. Turn it into one question with a visible answer
The answer must be something a camera can see happen: it starts or it doesn't, she says yes or no, the number goes up or down. "Is it good?" is not visible. "Does it survive the drop?" is.

Armstrong's titles are the question's setup with the answer removed. The title's question is first spoken 3–102 seconds in, and is still being answered at 94–98% of the video.

### 3. Give it a reason to fail and something to lose
- **A doubter or obstacle:** the brand that refuses, the deadline, the odds. Videos with one: ×1.21.
- **Something at risk** the viewer can count: money, time, pride, a relationship. Dobrik's drama words (CAUGHT, CONFESSED), which put a relationship at risk: ×1.62.

Without both, the answer feels certain and there is no reason to stay.

### 4. Package the question, not the answer
- **Thumbnail = the gap in one picture.** A person (so someone cares) plus the thing in its wrong state. Both creators: a face on one side, the subject on the other, no title text.
- **Title = setup + obstacle, answer withheld.** First person, capitals, 6–9 words.
- The face shows how to feel. Both smile: the promise is fun, not misery.

**Why a face works in a thumbnail, and how far that is known.**
- People look at faces first, and automatically, from infancy: in a crowded feed a face wins the glance.
- An expression is read instantly, and it tells the viewer what they will feel.
- A face that returns becomes familiar, so regular viewers recognise the channel before they read the title.
- A face beside an object gives the object a stake: this happened to someone.

The first two are well established in psychology. That a face raises the click rate is measured mostly by creators and tool vendors on their own channels, not in controlled studies, and nothing here was measured on click rates (only a channel's owner sees them). So treat the face as the strong default it is on both channels, and let the person's own click rate decide.

### 5. Open on the question
First sentence states the situation. Within 30 seconds a stranger knows what is at stake and who doubts it. No greeting, no logo.

### 6. Run small questions under the big one
Keep at least one question open at every moment; open the next before closing the current one.

Measured on three Armstrong videos:
- a doubt is voiced ("hopefully", "the problem is", "we don't know if") about **every 40–55 seconds**;
- the longest stretch with none was 2–4 minutes;
- a setback about every 4–7 minutes, a small win about every 2.5–5 minutes.

Dobrik does the same at a smaller scale: each bit is set up and paid off in under a minute.

### 7. Shape it: trouble early, wins late
In the three Armstrong videos, setbacks cluster in the first 60% and wins in the last 30%. The viewer is kept worried, then rewarded. An edit that front-loads the wins has nowhere to go.

### 8. Leave no empty second
Every stretch carries new information. Dobrik removes the empty parts (15–25 cuts a minute). Armstrong talks over them (145–200 words a minute, written after the edit). Pick one.

### 9. Pay off late, on a face
The answer lands at 85–93% and is shown through a real reaction, held without cutting away. It is among the most replayed moments in all three Armstrong videos, and Dobrik holds his for 50–90 seconds.

### 10. Close the account, open the next
- **Score it:** cost against value, before against after, the final count. A number makes the ending feel finished.
- **Open the next question** in one sentence.
- **Sign off the same way every time,** with a small extra after the end.

### 11. Repeat the frame
Same thumbnail layout, same title shapes, same skeleton, same sign-off. Viewers learn what the channel's question is and recognise the next one instantly. In both channels the opening episode of a new question draws the most (Armstrong: 13.0M against 8.6–9.9M for later episodes).

## Scoring an idea before filming or cutting

Score each 0–2. Under 7 of 12, change the idea, not the edit.

| Test | 0 | 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Gap** | Normal thing in its normal state | Familiar thing in a clearly wrong state |
| **Question** | No single question | One question, answer visible on camera |
| **Doubt** | Outcome obvious | Someone or something says it will fail |
| **Stake** | Nothing to lose | A loss the viewer can count |
| **One picture** | Needs explaining | The gap is clear in a thumbnail with no words |
| **Face** | No reaction on camera | A real reaction at the answer |
