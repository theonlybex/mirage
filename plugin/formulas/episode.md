# Episode formula (build story)

For videos that are episodes of a project: a build, a repair, a challenge told over several videos. Derived from Mat Armstrong's channel (latest 200 videos, 24 thumbnails, captions of 3 videos), measured 2026-11-09, and written so it works without his budget. The reasoning it shares with the other formats is in `formulas { id: "algorithm" }`.

Numbers marked "×" compare median views inside his channel; they show what goes with more views, not proof of cause: old videos have had longer to collect views, and big ideas get both the bold title and the views. Cadence numbers come from phrase counts in three videos and are approximate.

## The question this format sells
**"Will this broken or unfinished thing ever work?"** One project, one question, answered at the end of the series; each episode answers a smaller version of it.

## 1. The idea
- **A gap:** something the viewer knows should work, shown not working. The bigger the distance between what it should be and what it is, the better.
- **A doubter or obstacle:** someone who says it can't be done, a deadline, missing parts. ×1.21.
- **A stake that can be counted:** money spent, days left, attempts used.
- Low-budget versions: the cheapest one for sale, the one everyone gave up on, a deadline you set yourself.

## 2. The series
- 3–6 episodes per project: **get it → first attempt → the work → first use → the verdict.**
- The first episode draws the most (13.0M against 8.6–9.9M for later ones), so put the best gap and the best thumbnail there.
- Every episode ends on one open question that the next one answers.
- About one episode a week.

## 3. Title
- Capitals, first person, about 9 words.
- Four shapes:
  - "I BOUGHT / STARTED / GOT A [broken state] [thing]": ×1.35
  - "ATTEMPTING TO [start / finish / use] …"
  - "… IN [N] DAYS / HOURS": ×1.21
  - "[doubter] SAID IT WAS IMPOSSIBLE / REFUSED …": ×1.21
- Plain "REBUILDING …" with no obstacle is the weakest: ×0.82.
- State the setup; never the result.

## 4. Thumbnail
- One layout, kept for every episode.
- Your face on the left third, the same look each time, smiling.
- The thing on the right two-thirds, with its broken side facing the camera.
- A bright background that says where.
- No title words. At most one small extra: a price, a stamp, an arrow, a prop in hand.
- No second person unless they are the doubter.

## 5. The episode, in order
| Where | What |
|---|---|
| First sentence | The situation and the stake. No greeting. |
| First 30 s | Recap for a newcomer, using the doubter's own words. |
| By 1–2 min | "Today the plan is…", and the options weighed aloud with their risks. |
| The middle | Work, with a setback every 4–7 minutes and a small win every 2.5–5 minutes. |
| 85–93% | The answer: it works or it doesn't, shown with a real reaction and not cut short. |
| After it | The count: cost against value, before against after. |
| Last 20 s | The next question in one sentence, the same sign-off, a short extra (bloopers). |

## 6. Keeping people through it
- **Trouble early, wins late.** Setbacks belong in the first 60%, wins in the last 30%.
- **Always one open question.** Voice a doubt about every 40–55 seconds ("hopefully", "the problem is", "we don't know if"); never go more than 2–4 minutes without one.
- **Narrate after the edit, without gaps:** 145–200 words a minute. Explain each part in plain words.
- **Length follows the material.** His run 31–99 minutes, and longer ones do better (over 60 minutes ×1.28), but only because nothing in them is empty.

## 7. What matters most (my estimate)
Nobody outside YouTube can measure each part's true share. These shares are an estimate, not a measurement.

| Part | Share |
|---|---|
| The gap, the doubter and the stake | 35% |
| Title and thumbnail | 20% |
| The series shape and steady schedule | 15% |
| Narration with no empty stretch | 15% |
| The late answer and the count | 10% |
| Sign-off, extra, recurring people | 5% |

## Checklist before publishing
- [ ] The title states a setup and an obstacle, not the result.
- [ ] The thumbnail shows the gap with no words.
- [ ] The first sentence is the situation.
- [ ] A newcomer understands the stake within 30 seconds.
- [ ] No stretch over 4 minutes without a doubt, setback or win.
- [ ] The answer lands after 85%.
- [ ] A number closes it.
- [ ] The last line opens the next episode.

### Titles
- Capitals: 195 of 200.
- Length, latest 60: 5–15 words, 9 typical, about 51 characters.
- No "!!", no brackets, no emoji. A "|" part marker ("| PT1") only on old videos.
- "I BOUGHT" opens 31 of 200; "REBUILDING" opens 73 of 200, mostly older ones.
- What goes with more views, in his latest 60: "I BOUGHT" ×1.35 (13.0M against 9.6M); a deadline ("IN 7 DAYS", "IN 24 HOURS") ×1.21; the brand as opponent ×1.21; plain "REBUILDING" ×0.82; a price in the title ×0.92, so no lift.
- What he bought matters more than the wording: yacht, Bugatti and Rolls-Royce videos have a median of 12.0M; other cars 9.6M. His four lowest recent videos are all one ordinary series (a Lamborghini Urus for his girlfriend).

### The series
- Each vehicle is 3–6 episodes: buy, try to start, rebuild, drive, verdict.
- Median views by position, latest 60: buying episode 13.0M; middle episodes 9.9M; finishing and driving 8.6M.

### Inside the video (3 videos, captions)
- No chapters set on any of the three.
- Speech: 145–200 words a minute in every 5-minute block; 93 only in the final bloopers block.
- First words: the situation ("Here is the front half of a Bugatti Chiron…").
- A doubt voiced every 39–54 seconds; longest gap 131–250 seconds.
- Setbacks: 9–17 per video. Small wins: 9–27 per video.
- Most replayed points: 92–93% (the answer) in all three; also around the 8-minute mark in two.
- Closing words, the same each time: "Thanks so much for watching this video. If you enjoyed it, hit the subscribe button, hit the thumbs up button, and I'll see you in the next video. Peace out."
- Then bloopers over the same outro song.

### Not measured
Cut rate, camera angles, music choices, on-screen text, sponsor placement, upload days, and click or retention rates (only he can see those).
